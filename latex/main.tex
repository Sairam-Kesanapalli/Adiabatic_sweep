\begin{abstract}
Static CMOS dissipates energy on every charge and discharge of its load.
Adiabatic logic charges nodes from a ramped power clock and returns most of
the stored charge to it. We compare an Efficient Charge Recovery Logic (ECRL)
inverter and a Two-Level Adiabatic Logic (2LAL) inverter in the SkyWater
SKY130 process with one simulator (ngspice), one supply (1.8~V), one load
(25~fF per node), alternating data and one energy definition: the net energy
drawn from the power clocks per clock period. The ramp time is swept from 0.5
to 100~ns, and the device width, channel length and load capacitance are swept
one at a time. Over the width sweep, ECRL dissipates 11.55 to 59.59~fJ per
operation and 2LAL 0.62 to 50.30~fJ. At $W=1$~\textmu m, 2LAL dissipates
1.19$\times$ less than ECRL at a 1~ns ramp and 13.4$\times$ less at 100~ns; a
simulated static CMOS inverter with the same load dissipates 43.8~fJ per
transition. The two curves cross at a 0.66~ns ramp (65.3~fJ, above the CMOS
energy): 2LAL is cheaper for slower ramps and ECRL for faster ones. 2LAL
energy falls nearly as $1/T$ (log--log slope $-0.84$ to
$-0.96$ between 10 and 50~ns), while ECRL levels off at a threshold-voltage
residue of about 15~fJ. In the load sweep, 2LAL energy follows
$R(\CL+C_p)^2$ with an $R$ within 8\% of the measured transmission-gate
resistance; at a published 2LAL operating point, the SKY130 circuit dissipates
1.17$\times$ the reported energy per transistor. The saving costs speed: the
energy-delay product of static CMOS is 9$\times$ lower than the best adiabatic
value in the sweep. Two testbench defects that
reversed conclusions, a metering window without a data transition and an
unterminated 2LAL chain, are described and corrected.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background}
In static CMOS, every charge of a load capacitance $\CL$ from a constant supply
dissipates $\tfrac12\CL\VDD^2$ in the pull-up network, and every discharge
dissipates the stored $\tfrac12\CL\VDD^2$ in the pull-down network, regardless
of how slowly the gate switches \cite{chandrakasan1992,rabaey2003}. Lowering
$\VDD$ reduces this energy, but speed and noise margin limit how far.
Landauer showed that erasing one bit costs at least $kT\ln2$
\cite{landauer1961}, and Bennett showed that computation can in principle
be carried out without erasure \cite{bennett1973}. The $\tfrac12\CL\VDD^2$
per transition of CMOS is therefore a property of the circuit style, not a
limit of computation.

\subsection{Adiabatic Logic}
Adiabatic switching charges a node through a resistive path from a supply that
ramps slowly, so that the voltage across the switch stays small throughout
the transition \cite{koller1992,athas1994}. The dissipated energy then scales
with the ratio of the circuit time constant to the ramp time
(\cref{sec:adiabatic}), and the stored charge can be returned to the supply on
the falling ramp. Families differ in how completely they meet the two rules
of adiabatic switching: never turn a switch on while a voltage is across it,
and never turn it off while current flows through it. Quasi-adiabatic
families such as ECRL break the first rule once per cycle, when a transistor
turns on with roughly a threshold voltage across it, and so lose a fixed
residue. Fully adiabatic families such as 2LAL obey both rules and are
limited only by resistance and leakage \cite{frank2020icrc}.

\subsection{ECRL}
\label{sec:ecrl}
ECRL \cite{moon1996} uses four transistors and no DC supply: a cross-coupled
PMOS pair whose sources sit on the power clock $\phi$, and two NMOS
pull-downs driven by the differential input (\cref{fig:ecrl}). With
$\mathit{in}$ high, N2 holds $\mathit{out}$ at ground, so P1, whose gate is
$\mathit{out}$, conducts, and $\outb$ follows $\phi$ up and back down; the
charge on $\outb$ returns to the clock through P1 on the falling ramp. Thus
$\mathit{out}=\overline{\mathit{in}}$ and $\outb=\mathit{in}$, and exactly one
output pulses in every cycle, whatever the data. The clock is a trapezoid with
four equal intervals, named in \cite{moon1996} precharge-and-evaluate, hold,
recover and wait, and the input may change only during wait. A single gate uses one clock phase; a cascade places
successive stages on successive phases.

ECRL needs only four transistors and one clock per gate. Its main loss is
incomplete recovery: on the falling ramp, P1 stops conducting once $\outb$ has
fallen to roughly $|V_{\mathrm{th},p}|$ above its gate, which is at ground, so
$\outb$ is left at a residual voltage (0.53~V at a 10~ns ramp and 0.42~V at 100~ns in our
simulations). When the data changes, the NMOS discharges that residue to
ground, and the energy is lost (\cref{eq:ecrl}).

\subsection{2LAL}
\label{sec:2lal}
2LAL \cite{frank2003mistakes,frank2020icrc} is a fully adiabatic family built
from CMOS transmission gates (TGs) and clocked by four trapezoidal rails
$\phi_0$--$\phi_3$, each one phase later than the last; stage $n$ is clocked
one phase after stage $n-1$. Data is dual-rail: a pair $(T,C)$ rests at
FALSE $=(0,\VDD)$, and TRUE is a pulse on $T$ from $\phi_t$ while $C$ is
pulled down by the antiphase rail $\phi_{t+2}$. A logic 0 is the
\emph{absence} of a pulse. Encoding it as a pulse on $C$ instead makes the
pass-gate pFET conduct while $\phi_t$ is high and produces spurious ones
downstream.

Each cell has eight transistors (\cref{fig:2lal}): a forward pair of TGs
that drives the output pair, and a reverse pair that restores the previous
stage's nodes (from $\phi_{t-1}$ and $\phi_{t+1}$) once this stage's output is
valid. Our cell follows the PHAS2 cell of DeBenedictis's 4lc testbench
\cite{debenedictis4lc}. A four-transistor stage with only the forward pair
cannot pull its own output back down and latches high. Inversion cannot be
done by crossing the two rails either, because the rest state
$(0,\VDD)$ then becomes $(\VDD,0)$, a permanent TRUE. The inverter instead
uses quad-rail data: two dual-rail sub-chains carry $A$ and $\overline{A}$
and are exchanged at every stage. The exchange costs no transistors, but it
doubles the datapath, so one inverter column has 16 transistors. Because one
sub-chain always carries a TRUE, a column pulses once in every period, as an
ECRL gate does.

Every switch in 2LAL obeys both adiabatic rules, so there is no threshold
residue and dissipation keeps falling as the ramp slows. The costs are four
clock rails, four times the transistors of ECRL per inverter, and nodes that
float when FALSE and depend on the neighbouring stages to stay correct
(\cref{sec:termination}).

\subsection{Research Gap}
ECRL was introduced with an energy-versus-frequency evaluation of inverter
chains and a 16-bit adder in a 1.0~\textmu m, 5~V process with a reduced
threshold voltage of 0.2~V \cite{moon1996}, and 2LAL has been developed as a fully adiabatic family by
Frank and co-workers \cite{frank2003mistakes,frank2020icrc}, who have
reported simulated shift-register energies in Sandia's 180 and 350~nm
processes \cite{frank2020talk}. These results use
different processes, supplies, loads and energy definitions, so they cannot be
compared number for number, and an energy figure means little unless the
operation it covers is stated. We are not aware of a published simulation of
either family in SKY130; DeBenedictis's ngspice shift-register decks include a
SKY130 model option but report no comparison with ECRL \cite{debenedictis4lc}.
This work implements both families in one open PDK and one simulator, and
compares them under seven matched conditions: technology and corner, supply,
load, device width and length, time base, energy definition and switching
activity.

\subsection{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Implement ECRL and 2LAL inverters in SKY130 and verify their logic
        at every simulated point.
  \item Validate the energy measurement against static CMOS and against an
        exact adiabatic case.
  \item Measure the effect of ramp time (frequency), transistor width,
        channel length and load capacitance on energy per operation.
  \item Compare energy, average power, latency and energy-delay product (EDP)
        with a simulated static CMOS inverter, and identify where each
        adiabatic family uses less energy than CMOS.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{CMOS Switching Energy}
Charging a load $\CL$ from $0$ to $\VDD$ from a constant supply draws charge
$\CL\VDD$ and therefore energy $\CL\VDD^2$ from the supply. Half is stored on
the load and half is dissipated in the pull-up network, whatever its
resistance; the stored half is dissipated in the pull-down network on
discharge \cite{rabaey2003}. With switching activity $\alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}$
(full charge--discharge cycles per clock) and clock frequency $f$,
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{CMOS}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}\,\CL\VDD^2, \qquad
  P_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}\,\CL\VDD^2 f .
  \label{eq:cmos}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Adiabatic Charging}
\label{sec:adiabatic}
If the same load is charged through an on-resistance $R$ from a supply that
ramps linearly from $0$ to $\VDD$ in time $T$, the charging current is about
$\CL\VDD/T$, and for $T \gg R\CL$ the dissipated energy is
\cite{athas1994,fischer2004}
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{ad}} \approx \frac{R\CL}{T}\,\CL\VDD^2 .
  \label{eq:adiabatic}
\end{equation}
This is per transition; a full charge--discharge cycle costs twice as much.
The loss falls as $1/T$, and the stored energy returns to the supply on the
falling ramp. When $T$ is not much longer than $R\CL$, the exact loss of one
linear ramp is $\CL\VDD^2\,g(T,\tau)$ with $\tau=R\CL$ and
$g=(\tau/T)\,[1-(\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\tau})]$, which tends to $\tau/T$ for
$T\gg\tau$.

For quasi-adiabatic families such as ECRL, the recovery transistor turns off
once its gate--source voltage falls below its threshold, which leaves a
residue that does not scale with $T$ \cite{moon1996,fischer2004}:
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{ECRL}} \approx \frac{R\CL}{T}\,\CL\VDD^2
  + \tfrac12\,\CL\,|V_{\mathrm{th},p}|^2 .
  \label{eq:ecrl}
\end{equation}
In simulation the residue is larger than this single term, because the
rising output also lags the clock by about $|V_{\mathrm{th},p}|$ before the
pull-up turns on (\cref{sec:width}), and it shrinks slowly at long $T$,
because subthreshold conduction lowers the residual voltage (0.53~V at
10~ns, 0.42~V at 100~ns).

\subsection{Energy and Frequency}
Leakage adds a loss that grows with the time a node is held, so the energy
of a fully adiabatic stage has the form
\begin{equation}
  E(T) = \frac{a}{T} + bT, \qquad
  T^{*} = \sqrt{a/b}, \qquad E_{\min} = 2\sqrt{ab},
  \label{eq:ucurve}
\end{equation}
where $a$ collects the resistive term of \cref{eq:adiabatic} and $b$ the
leakage power. For a trapezoidal power clock with four equal intervals of
length $\Tph$, the clock (operation) frequency is $f_{\mathrm{clk}}=1/(4\Tph)$
and the transition frequency is $f_{\mathrm{tr}}=1/\Tph$. Plots use
$f_{\mathrm{tr}}$ unless stated. Static CMOS energy per operation is, to
first order, independent of $f$.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
\begin{align}
  \Eop  &= \frac{1}{N}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+NT_{\mathrm{clk}}}
           \sum_k V_k(t)\,I_k(t)\,dt, \label{eq:eop}\\
  P_{\mathrm{avg}} &= \Eop / T_{\mathrm{clk}}, \label{eq:pavg}\\
  \EDP  &= \Eop \times t_{d}, \label{eq:edp}
\end{align}
where the sum runs over every supply or power-clock source $k$, $I_k$ is the
signed current delivered by source $k$ (so returned energy is subtracted),
$N$ is the number of averaged clock periods and $t_d$ the delay of one
stage (\cref{sec:edp}).

\subsection{Energy Measurement}
\label{sec:meas}
Energy is the integral of the power delivered by the power clock(s) over
whole clock periods after start-up, so energy returned to a clock is
subtracted (\cref{eq:eop}). ngspice counts source current as positive into
the positive terminal, so delivered power is $-VI$.

\emph{ECRL.} A behavioural source converts $-V(\phi)I(V_\phi)$ into a node
voltage, which is integrated over cycle~3 ($[12,16]\Tph$, data $0\to1$, $E_A$)
and cycle~4 ($[16,20]\Tph$, data $1\to0$, $E_B$); $E_C=(E_A+E_B)/2$. The first
three cycles are discarded, and the same two measurements two periods
earlier serve as a settling check:
\begin{lstlisting}
BPCPOWER PCLK 0 V={-V(PHI)*I(VPHI)}
.meas tran E_A INTEG V(PCLK) FROM={12*TPHASE} TO={16*TPHASE}
.meas tran E_B INTEG V(PCLK) FROM={16*TPHASE} TO={20*TPHASE}
\end{lstlisting}

\emph{2LAL.} A 0~V source on each of the four rails measures its current. A
behavioural current source equal to $\sum_k V(r_k)\,I(V_{mk})$ charges a
1~F capacitor, so the capacitor voltage is the running energy in joules. Rail
periods 0 and 1 are discarded; $E_C$ is the energy of periods 2--5 divided by
32 (four periods of eight columns), and single-period values $E_A$, $E_B$
must lie within 1\% of $E_C$ before a point is accepted.

\emph{Calibration.} The same 1~F integrator was checked three ways. Driving
a static CMOS inverter with $\CL$ from 10 to 100~fF, the slope of energy per
cycle against $\CL$ must equal $\VDD^2=3.240$~fJ/fF regardless of parasitic
and short-circuit energy; it measures 3.250~fJ/fF ($+0.3\%$) at both 25 and
2.5~MHz. Driving an ideal 10~k$\Omega$, 25~fF load from the rail trapezoid,
where the exact loss is known, the meter agrees within 0.011\% at 1, 10 and
100~ns. Cutting the maximum time step by four and tightening \texttt{reltol}
to $10^{-6}$ moves no energy by more than 0.06\%.

\emph{Why the window matters.} The first ECRL deck metered a clock period
that contained no data transition. Holding the data static in the corrected
deck reproduces that value exactly (5.36~fJ at $\Tph=100$~ns); with
alternating data the same point is 12.46~fJ. In both families $E_A$ and $E_B$
agree to the last printed digit, so no activity correction is applied.

\subsection{Chain Termination (2LAL)}
\label{sec:termination}
In 2LAL each node is restored to rest by the reverse TGs of the \emph{next}
stage, so the last node of an open chain is never restored. Our first 2LAL
testbench was such a chain: a driver stage, four metered stages and one load
stage, whose outputs (node~6) had neither a load nor a successor. Node~6 then
drifted until both of its sub-chains read TRUE (about 1.67~V on $T$ and
0.11~V on $C$). That kept stage~5's reverse TGs on in both sub-chains, which
back-drove node~5 and in turn opened both of stage~4's reverse TGs. The
metered node $n_{T4}$, which should rest at 0~V, rose to 0.42~V at
$\Tph=10$~ns, and at slow ramps it snapped to 1.8~V, a non-adiabatic step.
The result was an energy inflated 3.4$\times$ at 10~ns (21.62 against
6.29~fJ) and 47$\times$ at 100~ns (44.05 against 0.93~fJ), with a plateau
of 44--45~fJ at slow ramps that coincides with the 43.8~fJ static CMOS
reference. The functional checks of that deck sampled only nodes
$a_1$--$a_4$ and $n_1$--$n_2$ and did not see the fault.

The corrected testbench closes the chain into a ring of eight stages, as in
DeBenedictis's 2LAL testbenches \cite{debenedictis4lc}. A ring
has no last node, needs no unmetered driver or load stages, and is
seeded with two data waves through initial conditions. As an independent
check, the open chain was also terminated with ideal sources carrying the
known data into node~6; that version and the ring agree within 0.8\% at all
42 width-sweep points.

\subsection{Functional Verification}
\label{sec:func}
\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\simfig{\columnwidth}{ecrl_inverter_T10.png}\\[2pt]
\simfig{\columnwidth}{2lal_inverter_stage_T10.png}

Logic is checked at every simulated point before its energy is accepted
(\cref{fig:waves}). For ECRL, both outputs are sampled mid-hold in both data
cycles and must lie within 10\% of the rails; at $W=1$~\textmu m and
$\Tph=10$~ns, $\mathit{in}=1$ gives $(\mathit{out},\outb)=(0.2~\text{\textmu V},
1.80~\text{V})$, and $\mathit{in}=0$ the reverse. For 2LAL, TRUE $=(\VDD,0)$
and FALSE $=(0,\VDD)$, and the ring deck carries 96 checks per run: in two
rail periods, every node's active sub-chain is sampled at the top of its
pulse and back at rest, and its idle sub-chain must stay within 10\% of rest
over the whole period. Which sub-chain is active at each node is predicted
from the seed, not read from the simulation.

Two further checks guard the checks themselves. A waveform decoder, which
never uses the predicted values to decide pass or fail, confirms at
$\Tph=1$, 10 and 100~ns that exactly one sub-chain of every node pulses in
every period and that every stage's output is the inverse of its input one
$\Tph$ later (42 of 42 stage-periods per run, and 154 of 154 over 20 rail
periods at 100~ns). This is the 2LAL timing rule of one tick of delay per
stage \cite{frank2017tutorial}. A mutation test breaks the ring in one place
at a time: each of six logic defects fails between 16 and 48 of the 96
checks, while the unmodified deck fails none. One defect, a stage that does
not invert, leaves the energy unchanged (6.29~fJ), so only the logic checks
can detect it.

\subsection{Parameter Sweeps}
Four parameters are swept one at a time about the nominal point of
\cref{tab:cond}: ramp time (and with it frequency), transistor width, channel
length and load capacitance. The width, length and
load sweeps are each crossed with all seven ramp times, and a further sweep
at the nominal point covers ramp times from 0.5 to 1~ns to locate where the
ECRL and 2LAL energies cross. Each point is a
separate batch run, and results pass from \texttt{.meas} to CSV to gnuplot
without manual editing. A point is accepted only if the substituted values
appear in the deck, ngspice exits without errors or warnings, every
\texttt{.meas} is present, and the device sizes and $\Tph$ read back from
the simulator equal the request. In the length and load sweeps, a point whose
logic fails, because a long channel or a large load cannot reach the rails
at the fastest ramps, is recorded as such and excluded from fits and plots.

\subsection{Choice of the Fixed Operating Point}
\label{sec:fixedpoint}
The length sweep holds $W=1.0$~\textmu m and $\CL=25$~fF; the load sweep holds
$W=1.0$~\textmu m and $L=0.15$~\textmu m. The reasons are these.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{It is the anchor of every other result.} Both sweeps pass through
  $W=1.0$~\textmu m, $L=0.15$~\textmu m, $\CL=25$~fF, and each sweep script
  checks that it reproduces the width sweep there; it does, to every printed
  digit. A change in a deck or in the models therefore shows up as a failed
  anchor.
\item \emph{A formula-optimal width does not stay optimal across a length
  sweep.} The adiabatic term behaves as $a\propto R\,(\CL+kW)^2$ with
  $R\propto1/W$, which is smallest at $W^{*}=\CL/k$: widening pays only while
  the transistor's own capacitance stays below the load
  \cite{fischer2003,fischer2004}. At $L=0.15$~\textmu m the width sweep gives
  $k=7.1$~fF/\textmu m, so $W^{*}=3.5$~\textmu m. The gate part of $k$ grows
  with $L$ (12.6~fF/\textmu m per \textmu m of $L$, \cref{sec:length}), so
  $k=7.1+12.6(L-0.15)$ and $W^{*}$ falls to about 1.4~\textmu m at
  $L=1$~\textmu m and 0.8~\textmu m at $L=2$~\textmu m. Fixing $W$ at
  3.5~\textmu m would turn the long-channel points into a measurement of gate
  capacitance.
\item \emph{The extremes are worse.} The minimum width (0.42~\textmu m)
  maximises $R$, so long channels would fail logic at more ramp times, and it
  is the smallest width the SKY130 pFET model covers. The largest width lets
  the gate load, which grows as $WL$, dominate $\CL$.
\item \emph{$L=0.15$~\textmu m for the load sweep} is the minimum length,
  which the length sweep shows to be the lowest-energy length at every $\Tph$
  except 100~ns, where 0.18~\textmu m is 0.8\% lower because it leaks less.
\end{itemize}
The 2LAL deck originally set the source and drain diffusion areas to
$1.77\,W L$, so a longer channel also enlarged the junctions. The diffusion
geometry now uses a separate length $L_{\mathrm{dif}}=0.15$~\textmu m; at
$L=0.15$~\textmu m the deck is numerically unchanged.

\emph{Energy.} Energy per operation rises with frequency in both families.
From 10 to 1000~MHz ($\Tph$ from 100 to 1~ns), ECRL rises 4.4$\times$
(12.46 to 54.69~fJ) and 2LAL 49.5$\times$ (0.93 to 46.10~fJ). Average power
$E f_{\mathrm{clk}}$ rises faster because both factors grow: from 31~nW to
13.7~\textmu W for ECRL, and from 2.3~nW to 11.5~\textmu W for 2LAL.

\emph{Dependence on ramp time.} The log--log slope of $E$ against $\Tph$
between 10 and 50~ns is $-0.92$ for 2LAL at $W=1$~\textmu m and between
$-0.84$ and $-0.96$ across the six widths. For ECRL it is $-0.24$ between 10
and 100~ns and $-0.40$ between 1 and 10~ns.

\emph{Relative advantage and crossover.} From 1 to 100~ns 2LAL is below
ECRL at every point, and the gap grows as the clock slows, from
1.19$\times$ at 1~ns to 13.4$\times$ at 100~ns. Extending the ramp time below
1~ns (0.5, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7, 0.8 and 0.9~ns, with every logic check passing)
shows where the two curves cross (\cref{fig:freq}): at $\Tph=0.66$~ns
($f_{\mathrm{tr}}=1.53$~GHz, $f_{\mathrm{clk}}=380$~MHz), both dissipate
65.3~fJ. For faster ramps ECRL is the cheaper family (71.6 against 82.7~fJ at
0.5~ns); below 0.5~ns both fail their logic checks. The crossover follows
from the two loss mechanisms. 2LAL swings twice the capacitance per operation
(two nodes, each up and down), so once the ramp is too short for adiabatic
charging its loss is the larger: the fitted energy scale $K$ of the exact-ramp
form is 224~fJ for 2LAL against 140~fJ for ECRL at $W=1$~\textmu m. ECRL's
threshold residue does not shrink with $\Tph$ and dominates at long ramps.
The crossing lies above the static CMOS energy: ECRL crosses 43.8~fJ between
$\Tph=2$~ns (39.82~fJ) and 1~ns (54.69~fJ), and 2LAL between 2~ns
(26.49~fJ) and 1~ns (46.10~fJ). Neither family beats CMOS at the crossover.

\emph{Agreement with theory.} 2LAL follows the $1/T$ law of
\cref{eq:adiabatic}: its $E\Tph$ product rises only from 59.6 to
72.1~fJ$\cdot$ns while $\Tph$ grows from 5 to 50~ns. Below 5~ns the product
falls (46.1~fJ$\cdot$ns at 1~ns) as $\Tph$ approaches $R\CL$, and above 50~ns
it rises (93.1~fJ$\cdot$ns at 100~ns) as leakage begins to count. Fitting
the exact-ramp form $E=K\,g(\Tph,\tau)+E_0+c\Tph$ to the seven points gives a
fixed term $E_0$ of only 0.11~fJ for 2LAL, 0.14\% of $\CL\VDD^2$, with
1.1\% RMS error. The same fit for ECRL gives $E_0=15.1$~fJ, 18.7\% of
$\CL\VDD^2$: the threshold residue of \cref{eq:ecrl}. Its leakage coefficient
comes out negative, because the residue itself falls slowly with $\Tph$ as
the residual voltage drops from 0.53 to 0.42~V between 10 and 100~ns.

Returned energy follows the same pattern. Between 10 and 100~ns, the share of
delivered energy returned to the clock rises from 95.1\% to 99.3\% for 2LAL,
but only from 62.9\% to 76.0\% for ECRL, because part of each ECRL cycle's
loss is the residue discharged to ground when the data changes. No minimum
of \cref{eq:ucurve} appears within the sweep: 2LAL energy still falls from
50 to 100~ns at every width, although the slope there flattens to between
$-0.26$ and $-0.87$. Locating $T^{*}$ needs $\Tph>100$~ns.

\subsection{Energy--Delay Product}
\label{sec:edp}
With $t_d=\Tph$ per stage, $\EDP=E\,\Tph$ (\cref{tab:edp}). EDP rises with
$\Tph$ in both families, so within this sweep it is smallest at the shortest
ramp: 46.1~fJ$\cdot$ns for 2LAL and 54.7~fJ$\cdot$ns for ECRL at 1~ns. The
cost of slowing down differs greatly. From 1 to 100~ns, ECRL's EDP grows
22.8$\times$ while its energy falls only 4.4$\times$; 2LAL's EDP grows
2.0$\times$ while its energy falls 49.5$\times$. Slowing 2LAL buys energy
nearly in proportion to the time spent; slowing ECRL mostly costs time.
Static CMOS, whose delay of 0.119~ns does not depend on a clock ramp, has an
EDP of 5.20~fJ$\cdot$ns, about 9$\times$ below the best adiabatic value in
the sweep. Adiabatic logic saves energy by spending time, and pays for it in
EDP.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{Why adiabatic energy depends on ramp time.} The loss is $I^2R$
integrated over the ramp, with $I\approx\CL\VDD/\Tph$. Doubling $\Tph$ halves
the current, quarters the power and doubles its duration, so the loss halves
(\cref{eq:adiabatic}). 2LAL comes close to this slope ($-0.84$ to $-0.96$
between 10 and 50~ns); ECRL shows a much weaker one ($-0.24$) because part of
its loss does not scale with $\Tph$.

\emph{Why load capacitance matters.} $\CL$ sets both the charge moved per
transition and the $R\CL$ time constant, so the resistive term scales as
$\CL^2$ at fixed $R$ and $T$. The load sweep confirms this for 2LAL, with an
$R$ and a parasitic $C_p$ that match independent measurements, and shows the
ECRL residue growing close to linearly with $\CL$ (\cref{sec:cl}). As a
result, the advantage over CMOS shrinks as the load grows
(\cref{sec:region}).

\emph{Why channel length matters.} A longer channel raises the
charging-path resistance roughly in proportion to $L$ \cite{fischer2004} and
adds gate capacitance in proportion to $WL$. The adiabatic term grows as the
product $R\,C^2$, so energy rises steeply with $L$ in 2LAL. Longer channels
leak less, but within this sweep the saving is visible only at the slowest
ramp (\cref{sec:length}).

\emph{Why width affects energy.} Width trades lower $R$ against more device
capacitance and leakage. It helps where the loss is resistive: short ramps
in both families, and 2LAL for ramps up to 20~ns. It does little where the
loss is a threshold residue (ECRL at long ramps), and it hurts where the
resistive term is already small: at 50 and 100~ns, 2LAL energy is lowest at
1.5~\textmu m and rises towards 3~\textmu m.

\emph{Practical differences between ECRL and 2LAL.} ECRL needs four
transistors and one clock per gate but leaves a residual voltage on the
released output (0.42 to 0.53~V here). Its energy per cycle also depends on data activity:
5.36~fJ with static data and 12.46~fJ with alternating data at 100~ns. 2LAL
needs eight transistors per cell, 16 per inverter column and four rails. Its
fitted residue is only 0.11~fJ, but its floating FALSE nodes make it sensitive to the stages
around the metered ones (\cref{sec:termination}); in this study that
sensitivity reversed the headline comparison until the chain was closed.

\emph{Comparison with published results.} Moon and Jeong report ECRL energy
per cycle rising with transition frequency for inverter chains with 0.2 and
1~pF loads, and, at 5~V and 0.2~pF, ECRL energy at 16\% of static CMOS at
10~MHz and 5\% at 500~kHz \cite{moon1996}. Our ECRL shows the same rise with
frequency, but it does not approach CMOS so closely: at the slowest ramp it
stays at 28\% of CMOS ($\Tph=100$~ns, $\CL=25$~fF). The threshold residue of
\cref{eq:ecrl}, relative to the CMOS energy $\tfrac12\CL\VDD^2$, is
$(|V_{\mathrm{th},p}|/\VDD)^2$: about 0.2\% for their 0.2~V threshold at 5~V,
but about 16\% for the 0.73~V SKY130 pFET at 1.8~V. The two results are
therefore consistent; the absolute energies are not comparable because the
process, supply, loads and device sizes differ. For 2LAL, Frank \emph{et al.} report
6.9~aJ per cycle per transistor for a shift register at 1~MHz,
$\CL=10$~fF and $W=480$~nm in Sandia's 180~nm process, and 37~aJ in their
350~nm process \cite{frank2020talk}. Simulated at the same point in SKY130
($\Tph=250$~ns, $W=0.48$~\textmu m, $L=0.18$~\textmu m), our ring passes all 96
logic checks and dissipates 8.1~aJ per transistor per cycle, 1.17$\times$ the
published value. A quad-rail column has 16 transistors and one pulsing
sub-chain per cycle, which corresponds to 50\% activity per sub-chain, as in
the published figure. \Cref{tab:prior} summarises these comparisons.

\emph{Conditions that favour energy recovery.} Recovery is favoured by ramps
that are long compared with $R\CL$, by switches that never turn on across a
voltage and never turn off while carrying current (2LAL returns 99.3\% of the
delivered energy at 100~ns), and, for ECRL, by low data activity, because the
residue is lost only when the data changes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
This is a pre-layout simulation study in one process corner (SKY130
\texttt{tt}, 27~$^\circ$C). It covers inverters only, with no extracted
parasitics and no fabricated or measured silicon. The power-clock generator
is ideal and its losses are excluded, as in most adiabatic results. No
supply-voltage sweep has been run, and the CMOS reference is a single
inverter driven with fixed 100~ps input edges. The ECRL gate is driven by
ideal slow-ramp inputs whose energy is not metered. The 2LAL cell follows
DeBenedictis's PHAS2 cell \cite{debenedictis4lc} and Frank's descriptions
\cite{frank2003mistakes,frank2020icrc}; it has not been compared with a
fabricated 2LAL circuit.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
ECRL and 2LAL inverters were compared in SKY130 under one simulator, one
supply, one load and one energy definition, and checked against a simulated
static CMOS inverter. 2LAL dissipated less than ECRL for every ramp from 1 to
100~ns, by 1.19$\times$ at 1~ns and 13.4$\times$ at 100~ns ($W=1$~\textmu m);
the two curves cross at a 0.66~ns ramp, above the CMOS energy, and ECRL is
the cheaper family only for faster ramps. 2LAL followed the adiabatic $1/T$ law with a fixed residue of only 0.11~fJ. ECRL
levelled off at a residue of about 15~fJ that tracks the pull-up pFET
threshold, and its energy depends on data activity. Both beat the 43.8~fJ
CMOS reference for ramps of 2~ns and slower at $\CL=25$~fF, and the region
in which they do shrinks as the load grows. The lowest energies were 0.62~fJ
for 2LAL ($W=1.5$~\textmu m, $\Tph=100$~ns) and 11.55~fJ for ECRL
($W=3$~\textmu m, $\Tph=100$~ns) over the width sweep. Energy rose with channel
length in both families, steeply enough in 2LAL that it lost its advantage
above $L\approx0.85$~\textmu m at 10~ns. Slowing 2LAL buys energy nearly in
proportion to the time spent, whereas slowing ECRL mostly costs time. At a published
2LAL operating point, the SKY130 ring came within 1.17$\times$ of the
reported energy per transistor. Finally, adiabatic energy figures depend on
the metering window and on the boundaries of the test structure as much as on
the circuit: two such defects each reversed a conclusion in this study.

\section*{Future Work}
NAND, NOR and multi-gate chains in both families; an ECRL chain in place of
the single gate with ideal inputs; separation of leakage from switching
energy by measuring at zero activity; supply-voltage sweeps; ramps longer
than 100~ns to locate the 2LAL minimum; layout with extracted parasitics,
where 2LAL's larger standing capacitance will show; and the cost of
generating the power clocks.
